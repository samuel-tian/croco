Introduction

Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) is a fundamental component of many Simultaenous Localization And Mapping (SLAM) systems, where it is used to estimate pose and trajectory by fusing camera images with IMU data. The inclusion of IMU data enable metric scale recovery, while the vision system mitigates drift caused bias and noise in the IMU sensors. State-of-the-art (SOTA) methods such as VINS-MONO and Kimera-Multi have shown robust performance, both on datasets such as KITTI, EuRoC, and Kimera, and in the real-world. However, a VIO system's ability to localize is severely degraded by occlusions, poor lighting, or motion blur. When failures in the vision pipeline occur, the IMU can still provide valuable motion data; however, without camera images to correct the drift, naively integrating noisy IMU data will lead to errors that grow exponentially. Additionally, existing deep learning approaches to inertial odometry, such as TLIO, are primarily trained on pedestrian datasets and generalize poorly. This motivates improving inertial-only methods for more robust SLAM systems.

Meanwhile, state-of-the-art end-to-end learning approaches such as DUST3R [10] / MAST3R [7], and VGGT [9], have shown impressive performance for tasks such 3D scene reconstruction, point tracking, and pose estimation for SLAM. This suggests that their training pipelines allow them to learn powerful geometric priors that could be informative for inertial-only navigation. However, despite the robustness of MAST3R [7] and VGGT [9], they still struggle in poor visual conditions and lack consistent metric scale. As such, we propose incorporating IMU data into a similar training pipelines to enhance performance across both modalities. Our two main questions

  • Can simultaneous image reconstruction and IMU sequence reconstruction help end-to-end vision models learn metric scale?
  • Can the geometric priors learned by end-to-end vision models improve the accuracy of inertial odometry measurements?

We introduce Alligator (Attentional Learning for Linked Image Generation And Trajectory Oriented Reasoning), a self-supervised training objective designed for learning joint embeddings for image and temporal IMU data. By utilizing masked sequence modeling, we show that simultaneously learning embeddings across multiple modalities has the potential to yield generalizable representations of visual-inertial data that can be used in a variety of downstream 3D vision and SLAM tasks.

Background

Pretraining via Masked Language Modeling (MLM), where a transformer is trained to predict randomly masked tokens in a text sequence by leveraging both left and right context, has been shown to be an effective self-supervised learning method (e.g., BERT [13]), with signficant success in many NLP tasks. Inspired by MLM, Masked Image Modeling (MIM) has emerged as powerful method for vision tasks, wherein a transformer is instead asked to predict masked patches of an image using unmasked patches as context (e.g., SimMIM[14]). CroCoNet [11] propose the pretraining task of cross-view completion, which builds upon traditional MIM by using both the unmasked patches and a second reference image from a different viewpoint as context to fill in masked patches. They show that leveraging both images leads to improved learning of 3D scene geometry, which has been key to enabling strong performance in downstream tasks such as depth estimation and optical flow. Finally, related work in vision-language learning [4] [12] combine both MLM and MIM, highlighting the potential of masked modeling for learning cross-modal representations.

End-to-end 3D reconstruction models like DUST3R [10] use pretrained vision transformers from CroCoNet to estimate dense, aligned point clouds from image pairs without requiring camera calibration. MAST3R [5] improves upon DUST3R by incorporating geometrical and feature matching. Their strong performance on 3D vision tasks such as scene reconstruction make them strong candidates for use as the visual front-end for SLAM systems. However, they operate on only two views and require ignficant post-processing to incorporate more. VGGT [9] improves on this with a feed-forward network that jointly predicts key 3D attributes across multiple views using DINO-pretrained features.

In parallel, inertial-only methods like TLIO [6] predict motion from IMU sequences via learned priors, but are sensitive to heading and orientation and fail to generalize across modalities. Buchanan et al. [1] learn IMU bias evolution with signficant improvements in generalizability across modalities, but require sensor-specific tuning and geometric supervision. Other methods such as AirIO [15] have seen improved performance on robotic datasets such as EuRoC, these methods fail to effectively learn the 3D geometry of the scenes.

To our knowledge, no prior work has combined IMU data with end-to-end 3D vision models. While image-based models can infer 3D scene geometry, IMU sequences also contain implicit 3D information. VGGT demonstrates that training on related tasks can improve performance; as such, we want to understand if combining visual and IMU data, inspired by combined MLM and MIM ([4], [12]) can enable calibration-free VIO and SLAM systems robust to failures in the vision pipeline

Methodology and Experiments

We propose a self-supervised pre-training task that leverages two temporally adjacent images of the same scene, along with the IMU data captured in between. The second image and the IMU sequence are randomly masked, and the model is trained to reconstruct them by minimizing mean squared error. Training images and IMU data will be taken from the Project Aria Synthetic Environments [3] dataset.

Dataset Generation


Figure 1: Selected image pairs from our dataset. The images are sufficiently different so that our image encoder can extract meaningful features, but both images also contain several of the same scene indicators so that the reference image actually informs the reconstruction of our target image.

The Aria Synthetic Environments dataset contains hundreds of thousands of synthetic 3D indoor scenes. A camera trajectory is simulated, and the captured visual data is available alongside a global pointcloud. Following the methodology used to generate data in CroCo, we wish to select image pairs with moderate “co-visibility”, which is a heuristic for image similarity that is roughly the percentage of the pointcloud visible in both images. No overlap between the two images would result in poor pre-training performance because the reference input does not provide sufficient context, while high overlap would hurt the quality of our embeddings because little additional information needs to be learned by the encoder.

To generate these image pairs, we randomly sample a reference image from the dataset. We then randomly sample a second image taken within 50 timesteps of the first image. The overlap between the two images can be computed by counting the number of visible 3D points the images have in common. If the number of overlapping points falls within a specified range, the image pair is added to our pre-training dataset, otherwise we continue sampling. We also place a lower bound on the time delta between the two images to minimize the chance that we add two near-identical images to our dataset. For each image pair, we also include the camera's trajectory between the timestamps at which the images were captured. IMU data can be computed by fitting the camera’s positional and angular trajectories to a k-degree polynomial and differentiating. Figure 1 illustrates some of our generated image pairs.

For our pretraining dataset, we ended up processing 28846 distinct indoor scenes from the Aria Synthetic Environments dataset, attempting to extract 50 image pairs from each scene for a total of 1342776 visual-inertial triplets. For some scenes, it was more difficult to find image pairs that satisfied the time delta and co-visibility requirements, so the average number of triplets sampled per scene ends up being a little less than 50.

Pretraining

Figure 2: AlligatorNet Model Architecture

Our AlligatorNet model augments the CroCoNet architecture to incorporate IMU sequence embeddings. Figure 2, adapted from CroCoNet, illustrates these changes. The image encoders are identical to the image encoders used in CroCoNet; both the reference image and the masked image are encoded using a shared-weight vision transformer. The IMU sequence will be encoded using a sequence of self-attention transformer blocks. During the encoding phase, image and IMU masks will be randomly generated. We mask 90% of the target image (following CroCoNet) and 40% of the IMU sequence.

The decoder is multimodal and should fuse the generated embeddings for both input images and the IMU sequence. Target masked image tokens and target masked IMU sequence tokens are reconstructed in parallel, and the reconstruction of Ideally, the tokens in the target image sequence should attend to both the reference image tokens and the masked IMU tokens. Thus, we implement a decoder block that utilizes a 3-way cross attention mechanism, in which we have the target sequence attend to the two context sequences independently, after which we fuse the output attention scores using a simple linear layer. Similarly, we have the target IMU sequence attend to both the reference image and the masked image. The decoder block replaces the cross-attention layer in CroCoNet’s CrossBlock with the aforementioned 3-way cross attention layer, and thus consists of a self-attention layer, a 3-way cross attention layer, and an MLP in sequence.

Figure 3: 3-way cross attention layer (left) and decoder block architecture (right)

Finally, we append two prediction heads to the output of the decoder. One prediction head takes the target image tokens as input and outputs a reconstruction of the masked image, and the second prediction head takes the target IMU tokens as input and outputs a reconstruction of the masked IMU sequence. For both the image and IMU sequence, we compute the MSE between the ground truth masked tokens and the reconstructed tokens, normalizing within each modality. Our final loss criterion will be a weighted sum of these normalized losses.

Downstream Learning: Depth Estimation

We adapt our model architecture for several downstream visual-inertial transfer tasks, such as monocular depth estimation and pose/displacement estimation, in order to assess whether our embeddings contained relevant information for complex vision tasks like end-to-end reconstruction and localization. This will allow us to systematically evaluate how well the learned representations generalize by comparing them with domain-specific architectures designed specifically for the target tasks.

To adapt AlligatorNet for downstream vision tasks like depth estimation, we remove the decoder and replace it with a DPT head. While retaining the current attention-based decoder backbone and simply adding a decoder head could potentially improve performance on downstream tasks, simplifying the decoder allows us to more effectively assess the quality of the embeddings. This approach isolates the evaluation of the embeddings themselves, eliminating any influence from additional structural learning that might occur after the embeddings are generated. If these simplified outputs prove comparable to those of task-specific architectures, it would offer strong evidence that our cross-modal representation learning approach can be effectively extended to end-to-end SLAM pipelines in the future.

We train our augmented model on NYUv2, which consists of several labelled video sequences with both RGB and depth visual data across a range of indoor scenes. We used the test-train split as given by NYUv2, which matches the split used by CroCoNet. This amounts to 795 images for training, and 654 images used for testing. In addition, we used the same hyperparameters and transforms as CroCoNet in their evaluation of NYUv2, to ensure fair comparison.

Results and Analysis

Figure 4: Pre-training loss over 13 epochs
We leverage MIT SuperCloud compute resources to pre-train AlligatorNet on our dataset adapted from Aria Synthetic Environments. According to the CroCoNet authors, training the original CroCoNet model for 400 epochs takes approximately 64 GPU-days on NVIDIA V100. Because the AlligatorNet decoder architecture is multiple times larger than the CroCoNet architecture due to the multimodal cross-attention mechanism, training our model until convergence is simply infeasible given our available time and computational resources--we estimate that one training epoch for AlligatorNet takes 11 GPU-hours. However, because our encoder architecture is directly borrowed from the CroCoNet encoder, we can initialize the encoder with the pretrained CroCoNet encoder weights, which would significantly improve low-resource training because the encoder is already capable of extracting some features relevant for reconstruction. Ultimately, we were only able to train our model for 13 epochs. The pre-training loss is visualized in Figure 4. While we observe significant improvements in our pre-training loss criterion, we unfortunately do not reach convergence. However, we believe these results are promising, and suggest that with more time and hyperparameter tuning, AlligatorNet could achieve, at minimum, comparable performance to CroCoNet.

Preliminary results are encouraging, even with the model’s limited training. Figure ? presents the model's reconstructions of two image pairs from the pre-training dataset. Although some visual artifacts are present, a qualitative inspection of the outputs suggests that the model has already developed a reasonable understanding of metric scale. This indicates that incorporating IMU data into the pre-training process helps the model learn embeddings that could be valuable for end-to-end 3D vision tasks. With extended training, we expect the model’s performance to improve even more. A more rigorous methodology for evaluating the quality of the embeddings will be outlined in the Future Work section.

Figure 6: Qualitative visualization of depth estimation examples taken from NYUv2 dataset

Evaluating our augmented model’s performance on the downstream task of depth prediction provides a more robust method for assessing the quality of our embeddings. Our network is capable of generating predictions that achieve >80% delta_1 accuracy on the test dataset, highlighting the potential of our method for improving learned embeddings for 3D vision tasks. We achieved an overall delta_1 accuracy of 44.3% on the test dataset; although this is less than CroCoNet’s, we only trained for 150 epochs, as compared to their 1500.

Conclusion and Future Work

Our preliminary experiments demonstrate that the proposed reconstruction model is capable of learning meaningful spatial representations, even with limited training. Despite the presence of some visual artifacts, the model shows early signs of understanding metric scale and producing structurally plausible reconstructions. These findings suggest that integrating auxiliary signals—such as IMU data—during pre-training can significantly enhance the quality of learned embeddings for downstream 3D vision tasks. While there is still room for improvement, especially in reducing noise and increasing output fidelity, our results highlight the potential of the approach and lay the groundwork for future improvements through extended training, hyperparameter tuning, and more rigorous evaluation.

Multi-view Stereo Reconstruction

If further downstream evaluation indicates that the embeddings are of high quality, we can extend our work to tackle more complex tasks such as 3D reconstruction. DUSt3R builds on CroCoNet’s architecture by replacing the unified decoder with two cross-attended decoders that predict dense point-maps, which are then combined to reconstruct a scene. Inspired by this, we could adapt a similar approach by modifying AlligatorNet to also predict point-maps—while incorporating IMU data into the pipeline to guide the reconstruction toward metric scale. This would address a key limitation of DUSt3R, whose reconstructions are inherently scale-agnostic. Achieving accurate, scale-aware reconstructions using our cross-modal embeddings would represent an exciting step forward for robotics applications.

Inertial-only Odometry

There is also room for further experiments to evaluate the performance of our embeddings for inertial-only odometry. By fixing the visual encoder and training a lightweight IMU‐only head on the shared latent space, we can evaluate whether the combined pretraining method allows for learning geometric priors that improve IMU estimation as well. Gathering datasets with longer trajectories would better show if the cross‐modal features reduce cumulative error even when no images are available. Success in this direction would highlight the robustness of our representations for navigation in situations where vision is limited or unavailable.
